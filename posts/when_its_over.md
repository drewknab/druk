I've held a number of programming positions over the last 11 years of being A Professional™. I've never had a project fail before.

<!--more-->

### Background

I've spent the last, nearly, five years working on a project. I left a perfectly reasonable position headed toward management wrangling the streaming arm of a collegiate athletics SaaS platform for a "startup" centered around automated banking agents. AI was still cool and hip at the time, it hadn't evolved yet into the regurgitated slop machine that it is in {CURRENT_YEAR}.

That project, effectively, ended this week.

Dreamed up during COVID lockdown, the idea was simple. Credit unions and community banks wereinterested in expanding into automated services and virtual agents for customers, but didn't have the resources to build their own from scratch. We could build an affordable off-the-shelf SaaS solution and sell directly to them.

When I joined there was a single client waiting to go live, and no viable MVP existed. I, and the rest of the team, put everything into that MVP. I know I drove myself harder than I ever had. One month to build a CMS and craft an AI driven agent to be interacted with over a phone. Late nights, bad pizza, and worse coffee. The stress took an immense toll on me, I ended up gaining some 50 lbs that year, getting on anti-depresants for the first time, and butting heads with the designated Systems Architect. Who is, by all rights, a good man who I respect a lot.

Unfortunately, it wasn't enough. The waiting client got cold feet, pulled out of the project as we were getting to the finish line.

We just kept building and building.

Within that first year we did secure a first client. They ended up shaping a large portion of the application to their preferences, then we signed a couple more.

Never quite broke even. Chugged along for four more years. Built a lot of really interesting software, learned a lot about telephony. Now, we're sunsetting all that work. Four years of down the drain.

### What did I learn

I learned quite a bit about building software as a startup. 

1. Your MVP doesn't need to be built to scale at the beginning.
    - Scale when you need to scale.
    - Your 100 users don't need triple read replicas because that's what a blog post from some FAANG company says so.
2. Simplify your services.
    - It's okay to have a single monolith at first.
    - You don't know what you need to 
3. Boring is better
    - JUST QUERY A DATABASE
        - You don't need an elaborate caching for 12 users. 
    - Don't try to learn a cool new technology when you have a gigantic looming deadline.
    - Get your project out to people as quickly as possible.
4. Elixir is a great language
    - It's incredible the amount of overhead and additional services it takes for other languages to perform what Elixir can in 100 lines via OTP.
5. Selling products is always more important than building software
